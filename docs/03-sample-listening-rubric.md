# Chapter 3: The Sample-Listening Rubric

*Your ears lie. Here's a rubric that keeps them honest.*

---

## Why you need this

Your brain will do one of two stupid things the first time you hear a narration sample:

1. If you wrote the book, **it will flatter you**. Hearing your own sentences read aloud
   feels magical, and "magical" masks "mediocre."
2. If you're tired and rushed, **it will judge harshly on trivial things** (the narrator's
   accent differs from how you heard it in your head) while missing quality problems
   (pacing, breaths, clicks).

The rubric below forces discipline in both directions. Use it on the **retail sample**
you've been offered — the same 5 minutes a stranger will hear before buying. Score each
sample **twice**: once the day it arrives, once on a *different day* (a fresh ear matters
more than any rubric improvement).

## The five dimensions (score each 1–5)

Print Worksheet 2 (or open it on the site) to tally scores.

### 1. Voice–book fit
Does this voice sound like it lives inside this book's world? Warm romance, dry
satire, clinical self-help, and cozy mystery each want a different mouth. Ask yourself:
*if I heard this voice with the cover facing away, would I guess the right genre?*

- **5 —** Feels inevitable, like the voice the book was *written in*.
- **3 —** Doesn't clash; could grow on the reader.
- **1 —** Actively wrong: jittery for a meditative book, dead-flat for comedy.

### 2. Pacing and rhythm
Words-per-second control. The narrator should vary speed — faster for action, slower
for ideas — and still land every sentence breath. Skill: does the reading feel
*conducted* or merely *steady*?

- **5 —** You forget you're being read to; the tempo of the text is the tempo of the
  audio.
- **3 —** Even and inoffensive; a bit mechanical or a bit rushed.
- **1 —** Racing, or slow enough that you'd finish the book faster by eye.

### 3. Character and dialogue differentiation
For fiction especially: can you tell who is speaking without the text saying so? Voices
should differ reliably by pitch, rhythm, or attitude — not just "this one is slightly
deeper."

- **5 —** Distinct, consistent character voices over minutes; no blurring between
  speakers.
- **3 —** Differentiation exists but sometimes slips, especially under emotion.
- **1 —** Everyone sounds like the narrator doing a slightly different accent of
  themselves.

### 4. Technical polish
Ear-level cleanliness. Listen on **earbuds** and once on **phone speaker**, the way real
shoppers will. Note breaths, mouth clicks (sticky pops), room echo, plosive thumps
("pop", "boom"), and clipping (harshness at loud parts).

- **5 —** Studio-clean; breaths are musical, not intrusive; no clicks or echo.
- **3 —** Occasional breaths or minor mouth noise; a room tone you can sense but ignore.
- **1 —** Fatigue-inducing: echoey, clicky, or that sibilant harshness that makes you
  want to take the earbuds out.

### 5. Consistency across chapters
Ask for chapters from the **middle** of the book (not just the polished opening).
Consistency is where most narration fails: pitch drift, character voice slippage,
volume differences between "chapter 1 recorded Tuesday with enthusiasm" and "chapter
23 recorded Friday-for-deadline."

- **5 —** Middle chapters, late chapters, and opening all match in energy, tone, and
  character voice.
- **3 —** Mostly consistent; small mood differences between recordings.
- **1 —** Feels like a different recording in some chapters; tension exhausted at
  chapter 12.

## Red flags — listen specifically for these

A "3" average is acceptable; any of these is a reason to walk away, not redeemable by a
cheap price:

- The narrator says a word wrong (or AI mispronounces) and you hear them *try again and
  recover* — or worse, don't re-record at all.
- Mouth clicks so frequent that a stranger's earbud session will fatigue.
- Character voices switch sides (a gruff character becomes plummy for a chapter, then
  gruff again).
- AI voices that audibly inhale-but-don't-breathe, or swallow plosives ("p", "b"
  mushed).
- Tempo panic — a bulleted list read as if they're being timed by a stopwatch.
- Long dramatic monologue read at, and only at, the same emotional temperature.

## Rubric math

**Total /25.**

- **21–25:** strong yes. Offer them the book.
- **16–20:** worth a second-guess. Consider a paid second audition of another narrator.
- **11–15:** not for this book.
- **Under 11:** no. Whatever it costs.

Human average expectation 18–22; AI voice expectation 14–18 (typically strong on
polish, weak on character). Track it in Worksheet 2.

## Two minutes that save you two hundred dollars

The rubric works on **any** sample — on the 5-minute retail clip the platform gives
shoppers, and also on a first chapter before you buy. Two listen-throughs with the
rubric is less than three hours of your life, and it's the exercise most likely to
prevent "I paid for it, I guess I upload it" resentment later.
