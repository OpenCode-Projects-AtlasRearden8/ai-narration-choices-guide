Chapter 4: Rights and Disclosure Basics

The boring-but-load-bearing chapter. Ten minutes that prevent a year of regret.

Not legal advice. This is plain-words education from Asdale Dynamics, LLC. For big money, multi-party deals, or anything a publisher's lawyer asks you to sign, talk to an actual IP attorney. This chapter covers the questions every self-publishing author should know the answers to.

Question 1: Who owns the audio?

There are two common ownership scenarios, and they are very different:

  • Work made for hire / assign-all-rights (typical PFH deals). You pay the narrator a flat per-finished-hour fee; the audio files are yours. You can re-publish, re-price, move them between platforms, and include them in a box set. The narrator usually keeps the right to use their own voice sample in their demo reel — that's normal.
  • Royalty Share / non-assign-all (typical ACX exclusive share deals). You never paid cash, so ownership is more entangled: the deal is usually exclusive, term-limited (often 5–7 years, historically standard on ACX is 7-year exclusive term for Royalty Share offers), and switching platforms mid-term is hard or impossible. You keep a revenue stream but not full freedom.

Plain-words rule: The more money you can pay up front, the more freedom you keep afterward. Royalty Share trades freedom for cash-flow; that is a legitimate trade, just make it on purpose.

Question 2: What are you licensing, and to whom?

When you publish on a platform, you usually license your audiobook — you don't sell outright. Three license dimensions to notice before clicking agree:

  1. Exclusivity — can this platform be the only store, or do you still sell elsewhere? (Chapter 2 compared them.)
  2. Term — how long must the license run before you can leave or renegotiate? Look for the words "7 years from publication" or similar for exclusive deals.
  3. Termination clause — how either side exits, and what happens to the audio (whether it must be pulled from other stores, whether payments continue during wind-down).

Read these three things even if you read nothing else in the agreement. Term and termination are where regret lives.

Question 3: When must you disclose AI narration?

Disclosure rules for AI-narrated audiobooks keep evolving; the direction of travel is more disclosure everywhere. The ground rules as of writing:

  • Platform-imposed labeling. Google Play and Spotify/Findaway label AI-narrated titles in their catalog; ACX's Virtual Voice titles are marked inside the Audible catalog. When the platform does the labeling, do not undo it — don't claim human narration anywhere in your own marketing.
  • Your own materials. Never call an AI narration "narrated by [person name]." If you write your own audiobook description, blog post, or family-forwards-it email and the voice is AI, describe it honestly: "digital narration," "AI-narrated edition," or the platform's own term. Accuracy costs you nothing and a "gotcha" accusation costs you credibility.
  • FTC rules for endorsements (US). Testimonials or "reviews" you paid for, or swooning blurbs from friends in exchange for anything, need clear disclosure. Same logic as affiliate links.

Plain-words rule: When in doubt, in the words you control, name the voice honestly. No reader has ever put a book down because the description was accurate.

Question 5: Privacy and your own materials

  • Don't paste personal info (your home address, family details, student names) into narration scripts or AI voice tools. Tools may retain text.
  • Prefer AI narration platforms that are honest about their data retention policy; check before feeding a manuscript into any tool.
  • Keep contract paperwork (PDFs, emails) in a folder you control — your cloud, not the narrator's — with copies of the signed agreement.
  • If you record an AI voice that's cloned from your own voice, additionally confirm: (a) who else can use that voice clone, (b) whether the clone can be deleted on request, (c) what the tool does with its recordings of you.
